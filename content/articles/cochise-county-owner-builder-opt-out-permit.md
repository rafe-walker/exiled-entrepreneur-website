The Cochise County owner builder permit, usually called the "opt-out," lets you build your own house on a rural parcel of four acres or more without county plan review or building-code inspections. It does not let you skip zoning, septic, floodplain, or state codes, and it does not make the county stop caring about accessory structures. I built a two-container home under it, and the pole barn and the ground-mount solar array each needed their own accessory-structure permit on top of the house permit. Here is what the program is, what it is not, and what the paperwork looked like.

## What the Cochise County owner builder permit is

The program is formally the Rural Residential Owner-Builder Amendment to the county Building Safety Code. It was adopted by the Board of Supervisors in 2006 after residents asked for an exemption for rural owner-builders during the county's move to the International codes; as written then it exempted owner-builders on four-acre-plus parcels from the Building Safety Code outright. In 2010 the exemption was narrowed to plan review and inspections, and 2014 language added that the permit does not exempt anyone from state and county building codes ([history summarized by Ground Party](https://groundparty.beehiiv.com/p/the-hidden-history-of-the-opt-out-permit)). County staff proposed repeal in 2017 and again in 2020; neither reached a vote. In October 2025 the Board went the other direction and proclaimed "Opt-Out Month" ([Herald/Review](https://www.myheraldreview.com/news/cochise_county/cochise-county-declares-october-opt-out-month-for-owner-builders/article_365aec4a-95e6-4956-a45d-6866e5eb36fb.html)), so the program is not going anywhere soon.

Eligibility, per the county's own 2020 work-session material ([Cochise County BOS, Jan 28 2020](https://public.destinyhosted.com/cochidocs/2020/WKS/20200128_1884/4977%5F012820%5Foptout%5FBOS%5FWS%2Epdf)):

- Unincorporated Cochise County, in an RU, SM, or SR zoning district whose minimum lot size is four acres or more.
- The parcel itself must be four acres or larger.
- You record a disclosure statement with the County Recorder noting that the structure was built under the opt-out.

My parcel is about five acres, zoned RU-4, which is the textbook case for this program.

The amendment text itself ([draft text as circulated to the Board, 2014](https://public.destinyhosted.com/cochidocs/2014/BOS/20141014_733/2062%5FDRAFT%20RURAL%20RESIDENTIAL%20OWNER%2DBUILDER%20AMENDMENT%20MAY%202014%20%282%29%2Epdf)) adds constraints people miss:

- Residential only. The home must be for the owner's occupancy, not for sale or rent; offering it for sale or rent within one year of completion is treated as prima facie evidence of a commercial purpose.
- One dwelling per owner every five years. The five-year limit does not apply to accessory structures or additions on the same property.
- Permits are valid 36 months, with one 12-month extension on written request if substantial progress has been made.
- Potable water must be available to the site, sanitation must satisfy the county health department, and smoke detectors are required.
- Mechanical, gas piping, and electrical work still have to meet the mechanical and electrical provisions. You are exempt from the county inspecting them, not from the requirement.

## Option 1 vs Option 2

There are two tracks, and the fee schedule and the certificate-of-occupancy consequences are different.

**Option 2 (full opt-out):** no county plan review, no building-code inspections, and you do not submit construction plans. The county's current fee line for this is $350, which "includes a single-family home and one accessory structure," up from $157 before the 2022 fee update ([Cochise County fee schedule, June 2022](https://public.destinyhosted.com/cochidocs/2022/BOS/20220621_2353/6490%5FBOS%5F7%2D21%5FFee%5FSchedule%2Epdf)). Option 2 is not eligible for a certificate of occupancy ([summary of county permit rules](https://www.jaspector.com/permits/arizona/cochise/unincorporated/)).

**Option 1 (limited inspection):** you submit two sets of plans (hand-drawn is allowed), the county reviews them, and you get limited inspections of mechanical, electrical, plumbing, and fire-prevention work. Fee is $850 (up from $325). Option 1 can qualify for a conditioned certificate of occupancy.

Which one matters for you depends on financing and insurance, not on the building. A construction lender or a homeowner's insurer may want a CO or inspection records. If you are paying cash and self-insuring the build phase, Option 2 is the one most people choose. **[VERIFY: which option Joshua filed under, and the fee actually paid]**

For scale: in 2021 the county issued 118 opt-out single-family permits, which generated $19,214 in fees, or 4.6% of all single-family permit revenue (same fee schedule document). This is a small program by revenue and a large one by share of rural homes.

## What is NOT waived

This is the part people get wrong. The opt-out "limits plan review and associated inspections" and "does NOT exempt owners from compliance with State, regional and local building" requirements, with zoning, health, and floodplain called out explicitly (2020 work session). In practice that means:

- **Zoning / site plan.** Your application still includes a site plan meeting the Zoning Regulations' site-plan standards (Section 1705 in the amendment text), and setbacks and height limits apply. Accessory structures must meet the same site development standards as the principal structure unless the regulations say otherwise ([Cochise County Zoning Regulations, definitions chapter](https://www.zoneomics.com/code/cochise-county-unincorporated-AZ/chapter_2)).
- **Septic.** Onsite wastewater is an ADEQ Type 4 general permit, and ADEQ has delegated that program to the Cochise County Department of Health & Social Services, including application review and construction inspection for systems under 3,000 gallons per day ([ADEQ–Cochise County delegation agreement](https://public.destinyhosted.com/cochidocs/2025/BOS/20250603_3028/8549%5FCochise%5FCounty%5FFinal%5FADEQ%5FDelegation%5FAgreement%5F10%2E14%2E24%2Epdf)). The opt-out has nothing to do with this. My septic was permitted and inspected separately. **[VERIFY: septic permit type/number and whether a percolation test was required on this parcel]**
- **Codes still apply.** The county has adopted the 2015 IRC and IBC and the 2014 NEC, with IRC Appendix Q for tiny houses ([Cochise County code adoption exhibit](https://public.destinyhosted.com/cochidocs/2021/BOS/20210727_2168/5976%5FExhibit%5FA%5F%2D%5F2015%5FICC%5Fcombined%5F%28Finalized%5F7%2E16%2E21%29%2Epdf)). Under Option 2 nobody from the county checks your wiring against the NEC. You are still legally supposed to meet it, and your insurer will care after a fire.
- **Floodplain.** If any part of the parcel is in a mapped floodplain, a floodplain permit is separate and still required.

## What a two-container home needed

The house is two 40 ft high-cube containers set side by side, giving a 16 ft × 40 ft footprint, on concrete pillars about two feet off grade. A 40 ft HC is 8 ft wide and 9 ft 6 in tall outside, with roughly 7 ft 8 in interior width and 8 ft 10 in interior height ([container dimensions](https://www.conexwest.com/shipping-containers-sale/40ft-high-cube-shipping-containers/amp)), so two of them side by side with the shared walls cut out gives a single room about 15 ft 5 in wide inside before insulation.

Permits and filings on this build:

1. **Owner-builder opt-out permit for the dwelling.** Application to Development Services with owner name and mailing address, structure location, work description, site plan, intended use, a statement that the owner is acting as general contractor, and the option selected. The county records the permit with the Recorder. **[VERIFY: application date, permit number, and whether the county asked anything container-specific such as foundation/anchor detail]**
2. **Accessory-structure permit for the pole barn / coop.** Required separately.
3. **Accessory-structure permit for the ground-mount solar array.** Also required separately. The array was built in 2022. **[VERIFY: whether this was filed as a building permit, an electrical permit, or both, and whether the county asked for a one-line diagram]**
4. **Septic permit** through the county health department, as above.

Note the tension with the fee schedule: the $350 opt-out line says it includes "one accessory structure." In my case the pole barn and the solar array each carried their own permit. **[VERIFY: whether one of these was folded into the dwelling permit at no extra charge, and what each accessory permit cost]** If you are planning a build with several outbuildings, ask Development Services up front which structure they will treat as the included one.

The utility shed that holds the inverters and batteries is under 200 sq ft. Accessory structures of 200 sq ft or less get a reduced setback in some districts ([SR district regulations](https://www.zoneomics.com/code/cochise-county-unincorporated-AZ/chapter_9)), but I have not found a published Cochise County square-footage threshold below which an accessory building needs no permit at all, and I am not going to guess one. **[VERIFY: whether the sub-200 sq ft utility shed was permitted, and what Development Services said about it]**

## Practical sequence

If you are starting from raw land in RU-4, this is the order that avoids rework:

1. Confirm zoning district and acreage on the Assessor's parcel record. Four acres is a hard floor.
2. Call or email Development Services (Building Safety Division, Bisbee; developmentservices@cochise.az.gov, 520-432-9300; permits run through the CitizenServe portal) and ask which accessory structures will need their own permits for your specific plan. Get it in writing.
3. Septic first or in parallel. The health department's site evaluation can change where the house goes.
4. File the opt-out application with a site plan that already shows the house, septic, well or water tank, and every planned outbuilding. Adding structures later means more filings.
5. Record the disclosure. Keep a copy; it will come up at resale.
6. Build to code anyway. The absence of an inspector is not a design margin.

## What the opt-out does not fix

It does not fix financing: most lenders will not fund a build with no plan review and no CO. It does not fix insurance: ask your carrier before you pour pillars. It does not fix resale: the recorded disclosure travels with the parcel, and a buyer's lender will see it. What it does is remove roughly a thousand dollars in fees and weeks of review for a cash build on rural land, and let you use container steel, straw bale, or earthbag without arguing with a plan checker. For me that trade was worth it.
